Overview

During my co-op at the Ontario Ministry of Transportation, I contributed to UX research and design for the Ontario Occupational Exposure Registry — a public-facing government platform that helps workers track and report occupational exposures to hazardous materials.

I worked within an Agile cross-functional team, conducting research, synthesizing user insights, and exploring how AI could be integrated into UX workflows to improve government service delivery. I also served as Student Lead, co-organizing team activities across three Ontario office locations.

Problem & Context

The Challenge

Government digital services often lag behind modern UX standards, creating barriers for the very people they're meant to serve. The Exposure Registry needed to be accessible, clear, and trustworthy — especially for workers who may be reporting sensitive health-related information and need to trust the system completely.

My Scope

As Jr UX Designer and Technical Analyst, I focused on the research phase: conducting user interviews, facilitating usability testing sessions, and synthesizing findings into actionable personas and journey maps. I also led an independent research stream exploring AI integration within UX workflows.

Research Approach

I conducted structured user interviews with three participants, capturing detailed transcripts in two parts per participant to ensure depth. Following each interview, I applied AI-assisted analysis to identify patterns across participants — a novel approach to qualitative synthesis that significantly reduced analysis time while maintaining rigor.

  • Conducted 3 in-depth user interviews (6 transcript sessions total)
  • Applied AI pattern analysis to identify recurring themes and pain points
  • Synthesized findings into user personas representing key audience segments
  • Created user journey maps to visualize the end-to-end registry experience
  • Presented research findings and AI integration recommendations to project leads

Think.AI — AI-Integrated UX Research

I led an independent research initiative exploring how AI tools can be ethically and effectively integrated into UX research workflows. The goal was to identify specific use cases where AI accelerates synthesis without compromising the human insight that makes UX research valuable. Findings were compiled into a formal report and presentation delivered to the team.

Key Findings

Finding 01

Users needed clear confirmation that their data was being securely handled — trust signals were absent from critical form interactions.

Finding 02

The language used in form fields and instructions assumed domain knowledge that many workers did not have, creating anxiety and errors.

Finding 03

Navigation between related sections was unclear, requiring users to rely on browser back-button behaviour rather than intentional in-app navigation.

Student Lead Role

Beyond my design and research responsibilities, I served as Student Lead for the co-op cohort. In this capacity, I organised team-building activities and co-led a hybrid cross-office event spanning three Ontario locations — coordinating logistics, facilitating remote participation, and ensuring an inclusive experience for all attendees.

This role sharpened my skills in stakeholder communication, cross-functional coordination, and balancing leadership responsibilities alongside individual contributor work.

Final Product

The live Ontario Occupational Exposure Registry can be viewed at the Ontario government website. The research and design contributions from this co-op informed improvements to the registry's usability, accessibility, and overall user experience.

Learnings

Government UX operates under constraints that private-sector work rarely faces — regulatory requirements, procurement processes, accessibility mandates, and multi-stakeholder sign-off. Navigating these constraints while still advocating for the user taught me to be both pragmatic and persistent.

The AI research stream was particularly meaningful. It gave me the opportunity to define what responsible AI integration looks like in a UX context — not replacing human judgment, but amplifying it.
